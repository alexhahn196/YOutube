[SEGMENT 1: THE MOST BEAUTIFUL IDEA IN ASTRONOMY]

[SCORE IN — warm, wonder]

Start with the world itself, because it really is extraordinary.

K2-18b orbits a small, cool red dwarf star, a hundred and twenty-four light-years away. It's a sub-Neptune — bigger than Earth, smaller than Neptune. About eight and a half times our mass, two and a half times our size. And here's the strange part: sub-Neptunes are the most common kind of planet in the galaxy… and we don't have a single one in our own solar system. We are studying a type of world we've never seen up close.

It sits in the habitable zone. It receives almost exactly as much starlight as Earth gets from the Sun.

[BEAT]

Back in 2019, the Hubble telescope made global headlines here — the first possibly-habitable planet with water vapor in its air. It was breathtaking. It was also, later, probably wrong: a closer look suggested that "water" was more likely methane. But the dream had already taken hold.

And one scientist gave the dream a name. Nikku Madhusudhan, at Cambridge, proposed that K2-18b might be a "Hycean" world — a single, planet-wide ocean of warm water, under a thick hydrogen sky.

[SCORE swells]

Picture it. A sea with no shore in any direction. A hazy hydrogen atmosphere. A dim red sun that never quite sets. If worlds like that are real, they could be the best place in the galaxy to look for life.

In 2023, Webb seemed to confirm the picture. It detected methane at five-sigma, carbon dioxide at three — and crucially, no ammonia. That exact combination is what you'd expect… over an ocean.

Buried in that same 2023 data was a fourth hint. A whisper of a gas called dimethyl sulfide — DMS. On Earth, DMS is made almost entirely by life; it's part of the smell of the sea. The team was careful: the hint was weak, not robust, and needed confirmation. Depending on how you calibrated the instrument, it hovered around two-sigma… or dropped below one. Below one-sigma is a coin flip.

[SCORE OUT]

Then came April 2025. The same team pointed Webb's mid-infrared instrument at that one transit — and announced dimethyl sulfide, and possibly its cousin dimethyl disulfide, at three-sigma. Madhusudhan told the BBC it was "the strongest evidence yet" that "there is possibly life out there" — and said he might confirm it within one to two years.

[BEAT]

That was April 2025. Right now, it's July 2026.

Here's the detail almost nobody noticed at the time. In 2023, when the news was smaller, NASA put out an official press release. In April 2025, for the "strongest evidence of life yet"… NASA said nothing. No release. Silence.

[SCORE — single low note, then cut]

[SCORE CUTS OUT]

When the world's space agency goes quiet on the biggest possible story… that's the first crack.

[SEGMENT 2: STRIKE ONE — THE SIGNAL WOBBLES WHEN YOU TOUCH IT]

[SCORE IN — tense, procedural]

To understand what happened next, you need one number: three-sigma. It sounds huge. It means: if nothing were there, you'd see a signal this strong by chance only about three times in a thousand.

But in physics, three-sigma is not a discovery. The gold standard — the bar that confirmed the Higgs boson — is five-sigma. Three-sigma is the level where you write a careful footnote. And even that three-sigma only held up against one specific, curated model. Compared to a plain flat line — no planet features at all — the signal was closer to two-sigma. Madhusudhan's own team said, in writing, that it was at the lower end of what counts as robust.

[BEAT]

Then the investigators arrived.

First, Rajdeep Taylor. He runs the simplest, most brutal test there is — no molecules, no assumptions. He just asks: does a flat line fit this data? And the answer comes back: yes. A flat line — a planet with no detectable features at all — fits Webb's mid-infrared spectrum about as well as anything. Reduced chi-squared: right around one. In test after test, the data preferred nothing over something.

[SFX: low impact]

Then Kevin Stevenson's team, in a paper with a title that doesn't leave much room for interpretation: "K2-18b Does Not Meet the Standards of Evidence for Life."

They measured the raw scatter — the random jitter between neighboring points in the unbinned spectrum. That noise: six hundred and sixty-three parts per million.

[BEAT]

The size of the feature everyone was excited about: about two hundred and fifty.

[BLACK — 2 sec]

The noise was more than twice the size of the signal.

[SCORE — cold]

And it gets worse. That signal depended on a choice — how you group, or "bin," the raw data. Stevenson's team showed that different binnings give you a whole potpourri of different planet spectra. Change the binning, and you can find almost any atmosphere you want. Under their own preferred scheme, roughly seven out of every eight analyses did not support DMS at all.

Their conclusion, in plain terms: this isn't the fingerprint of an ocean world. It's red noise. Instrument static, dressed up as a molecule.

[SCORE OUT]

---

[SEGMENT 3: STRIKE TWO — THE FINGERPRINT ISN'T ONE]

[SCORE IN — unsettling]

Okay. Suppose you set all that aside. Suppose there really is a feature. Would it even mean DMS?

Here's what Webb actually sees at these wavelengths: the signature of one small piece of chemistry — the methyl group. One carbon, three hydrogens. It shows up as a stretch near three-point-four microns and a bend near six-point-nine.

The problem? That methyl group is in dimethyl sulfide. And in dimethyl disulfide. And in ethane. And propyne. And ethylene. And methanethiol. It's in practically every organic molecule there is.

It's not a fingerprint. It's a family resemblance. It's like identifying a suspect from "he had two arms and a face."

[BEAT]

So other teams asked the obvious question: what else fits?

Rafael Luque's group combined all of Webb's instruments — near-infrared and mid-infrared together — and found "insufficient evidence" for DMS. And they found something almost funny: ethane fits the data just as well. In some reductions, slightly better.

Ethane. C-2-H-6. The gas you get when ordinary methane is hit by ultraviolet light. It's on Titan. It's on Jupiter. It's on Saturn. It is possibly the most boring molecule in the cosmos — and it explains the "biosignature" at least as well as life does.

Other groups piled on. Some found that plain propyne scored even higher than DMS. NASA's own reanalysis, led by Renyu Hu, found that adding DMS to the model improved it by almost nothing — and that methanethiol, or even nitrous oxide, fit just as comfortably.

[BEAT]

And then the detail that should have stopped the story before it started.

When those teams tried to model DMS and DMDS… there were no proper laboratory line lists for them. No one had carefully measured how these molecules absorb infrared light under the right conditions. Every retrieval leaned on old, room-temperature cross-sections — and had to assume they still hold in a hydrogen atmosphere, a hundred and twenty-four light-years away.

We announced possible alien life… using a molecule we hadn't finished measuring in the lab.

[SCORE OUT]

---

[SEGMENT 4: STRIKE THREE — EVEN THEN, IT WOULDN'T BE LIFE]

[SCORE IN — low, final]

Now the last blow. Forget the noise. Forget the ambiguity. Pretend, for one minute, that the DMS is really there.

Would that prove life?

For years, the assumption was yes — because on Earth, DMS comes from biology. But in the last two years, that assumption collapsed.

In 2024, the Rosetta spacecraft's data revealed dimethyl sulfide in the gas around comet 67P — a dead ball of ice and dust. No ocean. No plankton. No life.

Then it was found drifting in interstellar space. Then it was made in the lab, from pure chemistry, no biology anywhere.

[BEAT]

And here's the part that quietly breaks the whole story from the inside. A photochemistry team — Shang-Min Tsai and colleagues — worked out what it would actually take. To produce the amount of DMS being claimed, K2-18b would need a biosphere pumping out sulfur about twenty times faster than Earth's entire ocean.

And a biosphere that loud, worked over by ultraviolet light, would flood the atmosphere with ethane.

[SFX: soft impact]

Ethane isn't there.

The story doesn't just lack evidence. It contradicts itself.

[SCORE OUT]

---

[SEGMENT 5: THE STEELMAN]

[SCORE IN — warm, fair — no irony]

Now stop. Because it would be easy, and cheap, to turn this into a takedown of one scientist. It isn't one. And that matters.

Nikku Madhusudhan did not lie. He reported three-sigma — and he said, out loud, that three-sigma is not a detection. He asked for scrutiny. The press didn't listen. That is not the same crime.

He hasn't retracted, and on the evidence, he doesn't have to. His argument is that the mid-infrared data carries real information the critics underweight — and he's right that only more telescope time will settle it. That's not denial. That's a genuine, testable position.

And here's what's easy to miss in all the noise: the search produced something real.

NASA's follow-up — four new transits of the planet, analyzed independently by three separate teams — robustly confirmed methane and carbon dioxide. And it pointed to something remarkable underneath: an interior that may be genuinely water-rich, possibly up to half the planet's mass in water, with no water vapor in the upper air, because it's frozen out in a cold trap.

That same study is exactly why we should be careful. When those teams scanned a library of hundreds of molecules, they found "moderate" evidence — that same two-point-seven-sigma level — for numerous gases at once, including some no one thinks is real. When your method lights up for a dozen random molecules, that's not a detection. That's the noise floor. For DMS specifically, the honest word from that analysis was: marginal.

The system worked. Not because someone was a villain — but because everyone did their job.

[SCORE OUT]

[MID-ROLL 2]

[SEGMENT 6: WE LISTENED]

[SCORE IN — vast, quiet]

There's a coda to this that almost no one has heard.

While the chemists argued, two of the biggest radio observatories on Earth — the Very Large Array in New Mexico, and MeerKAT in South Africa — turned toward K2-18b and simply listened. Not for chemistry. For a broadcast. For a transmitter. For anyone.

They listened across a huge range of frequencies. They listened for thirty-three days — one full year on K2-18b. Their instruments were sensitive enough to hear signals fainter than any earlier search of this world could have caught.

They logged millions of candidate signals.

[BEAT]

Every single one was us. Aircraft. Satellites. Radar. Human static, bouncing back off our own sky.

From K2-18b itself — across thirty-three days, across billions of channels — nothing.

[BLACK — 2 sec]

And the sea was silent.

[SCORE OUT]

---

[SEGMENT 7: THE VERDICT]

[SCORE IN — resolving]

So. Signal, or noise?

Let's clear the table, one piece at a time.

The alien signal — the dimethyl sulfide — does not survive independent analysis. Multiple teams, using the same data and more, find no statistically significant detection.

The number behind the headline — three-sigma — was never a discovery to begin with, and against a flat line, barely two.

The fingerprint isn't unique — plain ethane fits as well or better.

The molecule isn't a biosignature anyway — dead comets make it.

And the ocean world it supposedly lives on is itself unproven — it could just as easily be a lifeless mini-Neptune, or a magma world too hot for anything.

[BEAT]

So the meter lands here. Mostly Noise. The headlines ran fifteen months ahead of the data.

[SCORE — turn]

But here's the twist. This was not a failure.

Watch what actually happened. A bold claim was made — in public. And within fifteen months, in the open, a dozen independent teams tested it, pulled it apart, and published every crack. No cover-up. No committee. Just the fastest immune response in the history of science, working exactly as designed.

The system didn't break. The system worked.

---

[SEGMENT 8: THE PAYOFF]

[SCORE IN — building, hopeful]

And look at what we walked away with.

Before this fight, no one had carefully mapped the red noise in Webb's mid-infrared instrument. Now we have. Before this, almost no one outside the field understood the "methyl-group trap" — that one smear of carbon and hydrogen that can fake half a dozen molecules. Now everyone does. Before this, there were no proper laboratory spectra for DMS and DMDS. As of 2026, there are — measured, finally, because this fight forced the question.

And most important: we now know, in hard detail, what a real detection has to look like. Not one faint gas. Not three-sigma. It will take a strong signal — five-sigma — of several gases at once, in a combination no dead chemistry can fake, on a world we actually understand.

K2-18b didn't give us life.

It gave us the instruction manual for finding it.

[SCORE OUT]

Is anything alive under that sky?
