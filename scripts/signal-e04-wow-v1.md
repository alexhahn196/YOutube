[SEGMENT 2: THE NIGHT — August 15, 1977]

[SCORE IN — warm, nostalgisch, Band-Rauschen]

Late on the night of August 15th, 1977, nobody in the state of Ohio was listening to the sky.

The Big Ear radio telescope — a strange, beautiful machine, two giant metal reflectors facing each other across a ground plane larger than three football fields — ran unattended, as it did every night. It couldn't move. It couldn't track. It simply stared at one strip of sky and let the Earth's rotation carry the heavens past its ear.

And at some point that night, for seventy-two seconds, something poured through its beam — more than thirty times louder than the background hiss of the universe.

No alarm went off. A computer compressed those seventy-two seconds into six characters on a printout: 6… E… Q… U… J… 5. A code for loudness, rising and falling. Then the machine moved on.

[ON SCREEN: Intensitätsskala erklärt — 0–9, dann A–Z; „U" = >30× Hintergrund]

[BEAT]

It was quite a week on this planet. The next day, Elvis Presley died. Five days after the signal, a machine called Voyager 2 — you met it in our last episode — left Earth forever.

And a few days later, at his home, an unpaid volunteer named Jerry Ehman sat down with the printouts… and stopped.

[SCORE — single cold note]

[SEGMENT 3: WHY THIS ONE WAS DIFFERENT]

[SCORE IN — tense, procedural]

Here's why those six characters made a careful man reach for a red pen.

First: the frequency. The signal came in next to fourteen-twenty megahertz — the hydrogen line, the natural hum of the most abundant atom in the universe. Two physicists had predicted, back in 1959, that if a civilization ever wanted to be found, this is the channel it would choose — the one frequency every radio astronomer in the galaxy is guaranteed to be watching.

Second: that band is *protected*. By international agreement, no one on Earth — no satellite, no aircraft, no radio station — was allowed to transmit there.

[ON SCREEN: Cocconi & Morrison, Nature 1959 — „Searching for Interstellar Communications"]

And the signal wasn't sloppy. It was squeezed into a single receiver channel just ten kilohertz wide — a pinprick on the dial. Nature is rarely that precise. Transmitters are.

Third: the shape. The signal rose for thirty-six seconds and fell for thirty-six — a perfect bell curve, exactly what a source fixed among the stars should trace as Earth's rotation swept the beam across it. A plane, a satellite, a truck radio — none of them draws that curve. Ehman checked the fit himself: better than ninety-nine percent.

[ON SCREEN: 6EQUJ5 als Gauß-Kurve über der Antennen-Charakteristik]

And fourth — the part that turned a marvel into a mystery. Big Ear had *two* ears: two feed horns, sweeping the same sky minutes apart. A steady star-fixed source should have appeared in both.

It appeared in one.

[BEAT]

Hold on to that silence in the second horn. It decides everything.

[SEGMENT 4: THE HUNT — 49 years of nothing]

[SCORE IN — driving, search montage]

What do you do with seventy-two perfect seconds? You go back.

Big Ear stayed locked on that strip of sky for roughly the next sixty days. Nothing. It came back to it for years. Nothing.

Then the big guns came. The META array. A twelve-meter dish at Green Bank. In the mid-nineties, the Very Large Array — vastly more sensitive than Big Ear ever was. In 1999, a twenty-six-meter telescope in Tasmania, six sessions, fourteen hours each. In 2022, Breakthrough Listen — the best-funded alien hunt in history.

[ON SCREEN: Follow-up-Liste — META '87/'89 · VLA '95–96 · Hobart '99 · Breakthrough Listen '22 — alle: null]

Nothing. Not once. Big Ear kept surveying that sky for more than two decades — the longest SETI search ever run. Against all those years of listening, seventy-two seconds is roughly one moment in ten million.

And they stand alone.

[BEAT]

And the telescope itself? In 1998, developers bought the land and tore Big Ear down…

…to expand a golf course.

Half a century of headlines have sold those six characters as *proof*: proof of contact, proof we're not alone, proof the government knows.

In 2012, we even *answered*. On the signal's thirty-fifth anniversary, the Arecibo dish beamed a reply toward the stars — ten thousand crowdsourced messages. Aimed at three stars… none of which sits where the signal actually came from. And partway into the first transmission, the transmitter overheated.

That's us. That's humanity answering the phone.

[SEGMENT 6: VERDICT, QUESTION ONE — is it PROOF?]

[SCORE IN — verdict theme]

Question one. Is the Wow! signal proof of alien contact?

Here's what proof looks like. In 2019, a telescope in Australia caught a narrowband signal while pointed at Proxima Centauri — our nearest star. It looked spectacular. And then the verification machine switched on: months of forensics, thirty-nine hours of re-observation, hunting the signal's fingerprints in the electronics. Verdict, 2021: interference. Human-made. Case closed, in public, with the receipts.

[ON SCREEN: BLC1 — Sheikh et al., Nature Astronomy 2021 — „verification framework"]

*That* is the standard. A real detection must repeat, and it must be confirmed by someone else's instrument.

The Wow! signal was caught by one horn, of one telescope, one time. The second horn, minutes later: silence. Every follow-up for forty-nine years: silence. There is nothing to verify — only a memory on paper.

[BEAT]

So. Signal, or noise?

[BEAT]

On the question the headlines sell — *proof* of alien contact — the needle isn't in the middle. **Noise.** Not because aliens are impossible. Because *proof* has a definition, and one loud moment doesn't meet it. The man who wrote "Wow!" said it best, in his own words: he chose not to draw, quote, "vast conclusions from half-vast data."

[ON SCREEN: VERDICT-METER — Nadel tief in NOISE · darunter klein: „on the claim of PROOF"]

And here's what would flip that verdict overnight: catch it *twice*. One repeat — anywhere in that patch of sky, by two independent instruments — and this becomes the biggest story in the history of our species. The telescopes are pointed. The rules for that moment were rewritten just last year.

And that's the ten-second test I promised: ***"Did anyone catch it twice?"*** If the answer is no — you don't have a discovery. You have a story.

[BEAT]

But stay with me. Because this is where tonight breaks the pattern of every episode we've made.

[SEGMENT 7: QUESTION TWO — the explanations die too]

[SCORE IN — awe, unresolved]

Question two. Fine — not proof. Then *what was it?*

On K2-18b, the boring explanation fit better than the aliens. At Voyager 1, the boring explanation fit perfectly. That's how these stories usually end.

Not this one. Watch what happens when the boring explanations take the same tests.

*"It was a comet."* In 2017, that headline went around the world — mystery solved. Except: the comet was fifty-five minutes away from the beam that night, in the wrong part of the sky. Comets have never been seen shining at the hydrogen line at all. And a slow, drifting comet would have appeared in both horns. The observatory's own director took the idea apart in public. Rejected.

[ON SCREEN: Kometen-Hypothese (Paris 2017) — Widerlegung: Position (Dixon) · keine 21-cm-Emission · Zwei-Horn-Test]

*"It was Earth interference — a stray transmitter, a reflection off space junk."* On the one band no one was allowed to touch — and drawn as a perfect celestial bell curve? Ehman himself floated the reflection idea in the nineties… and then walked away from it: the mirror in the sky it would require was, in his analysis, unrealistic.

*"It was that Sun-like star everyone tweeted about."* Searched. In 2022, Breakthrough Listen pointed the best gear on Earth at it. Ruled out as an ongoing transmitter.

[BEAT]

Every mundane suspect dies under the same tests that killed the alien *proof*.

Forty-nine years. One perfect signal. Zero surviving explanations. That has never happened on this channel before.

[SEGMENT 8: THE NEW SUSPECT — the case reopens]

[SCORE IN — discovery, momentum]

Which brings us to the part almost nobody has covered.

In 2020, a team at the Arecibo observatory in Puerto Rico recorded months of sky scans — and within the year, that great dish collapsed for good. Years later, an astronomer named Abel Méndez went digging through them… and found little ghosts of the Wow! signal. The same kind of narrowband spike, near the same hydrogen frequency — but about a hundred times weaker. And they lined up with something real: cold clouds of hydrogen gas, drifting between the stars.

[ON SCREEN: „Arecibo Wow!" — Méndez et al. 2024/2025 (Preprints) — cold hydrogen clouds]

His hypothesis — and it is a hypothesis, not a verdict: the Wow! signal was one of those clouds, caught in the split second a violent event behind it — perhaps a flare from a magnetar, a dead star with the strongest magnetic fields we know of — pumped it up like a natural laser. Nature, impersonating a beacon, on the one frequency we watch.

It would explain the frequency. The single flash. Maybe even the silent second horn.

And if you're wondering what the signal *said* — here's the humbling part. Big Ear couldn't have kept a message even if one was there. It recorded a single loudness number every twelve seconds. If someone spoke that night, we saved the volume… and lost the words.

What it doesn't have yet… is its cloud, caught in the act. In 2025, the team re-analyzed the original Ohio tapes: the signal came out brighter than the old estimates, its position narrowed, its exact frequency revised. The trail, after half a century, is *warm*.

[BEAT]

And one more thing sits in that archive. This June, veterans of the original Big Ear team — the same men from 1977 — published a first map of their old survey: it covered most of the radio sky… and logged more than *forty thousand* unexplained narrowband blips that no one has ever examined. None so far match Wow!'s full fingerprint. But nobody has looked closely. Yet.

[SCORE — swell, restrained]

Seventy-two seconds is only how long our ear lingered — the time it took Earth's rotation to sweep the beam past that point in the sky. The signal itself? The math allows anything from seventy-two seconds… to seventy-seven minutes.

We didn't hear a flash. We walked past an open window — and caught half a sentence.

The telescope is a golf course. The dish that answered is rubble in Puerto Rico. But the printout survives, one team says the archive may yield a complete answer within the next year or two — and forty thousand unexamined blips are waiting in a filing cabinet in Ohio.

The case isn't cold. It just waited for us to come back.
