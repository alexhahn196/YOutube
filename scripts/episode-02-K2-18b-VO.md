## 2. DAS SKRIPT (Voiceover, Englisch)

[SEGMENT: COLD OPEN / HOOK]

In April 2025, headlines around the world said almost the same thing: astronomers had found the strongest sign of alien life yet — on a distant ocean world called K2-18b. A telescope in space had, supposedly, detected a gas that on Earth is made almost entirely by living things. "The first hint of an inhabited planet." "A discovery for the history books." The story reached hundreds of millions of people in days.

And then something quieter happened. One team after another went back to the very same data — and couldn't find the signal.

So what really happened out there, a hundred and twenty-four light-years away? Did James Webb find life… or did we find what we wanted to find?

This is K2-18b. And this… is SIGNAL.

[INTRO STING]

---

[SEGMENT 1: THE CLAIM]

Here's the claim that set the internet on fire.

K2-18b is a world unlike anything in our solar system. It orbits a small, cool red dwarf star in the constellation Leo, about a hundred and twenty-four light-years away. Astronomers call it a sub-Neptune — bigger than Earth, smaller than Neptune. Around eight and a half times Earth's mass, and about two and a half times its size. And crucially, it sits in its star's habitable zone — the region where a planet gets roughly the same warmth Earth receives from the Sun.

For years, K2-18b has been a magnet for headlines. Back in 2019, the Hubble telescope made global news by detecting what looked like water vapor in its atmosphere — "the first potentially habitable water world." Later work suggested that signal might actually have been methane. But the legend was already born.

Then came the big idea. A Cambridge astrophysicist named Nikku Madhusudhan proposed that K2-18b might be a "Hycean" world — a planet with a deep, warm ocean of liquid water, wrapped in a thick hydrogen atmosphere. An alien sea, under an alien sky. If such worlds are real, they could be some of the best places in the galaxy to search for life.

Picture it: a warm, planet-wide sea with no shore in any direction, beneath a hazy sky of hydrogen, lit by a dim red sun. It is a genuinely beautiful idea. And that is part of the danger — because a beautiful idea is exactly the kind we most want to be true.

And in 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope seemed to deliver. Webb detected methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18b's air — but not ammonia. To Madhusudhan's team, that exact combination was a fingerprint… of an ocean world.

Then came the headline that changed everything. In April 2025, the same team announced that Webb's mid-infrared instrument had picked up something more: a gas called dimethyl sulfide — DMS — and possibly its chemical cousin, dimethyl disulfide. On Earth, DMS is produced overwhelmingly by life — by ocean plankton. It is, quite literally, part of the smell of the sea. The team reported the signal at "three-sigma" confidence and called it the strongest evidence yet for a possible sign of life beyond the solar system.

The world heard three words: signs of life. Millions of views. Breathless headlines. So — is it true?

Before we weigh it: if you want the honest version of stories like this — the hype stripped away, just the evidence — that's this whole channel. Subscribe, because we follow these claims all the way to the truth, every single time.

[MID-ROLL 1]

---

[SEGMENT 2: THE EVIDENCE]

Okay. Let's look at what Webb actually measured — because the real story is more interesting than the headline, and a lot more humbling.

Start with the planet itself. Is K2-18b even an ocean world? Here's the uncomfortable truth: we don't know. The exact same Webb data can be explained at least three completely different ways. One: Madhusudhan's Hycean world — a habitable ocean under hydrogen. Two: a gas-rich mini-Neptune with no solid surface at all — just a deep, crushing atmosphere. Three: a scorched world sitting on an ocean of molten magma, far too hot for anything alive. Independent teams have published all three. Some climate models even suggest that a stable, temperate ocean on K2-18b would be hard to maintain — the planet could tip into a runaway steam greenhouse. So before we even reach the word "life," the "habitable ocean" itself is just one interpretation among several.

Now, the star of the show: the dimethyl sulfide. And to understand it, you need one number — three-sigma. It sounds impressive. It means: if there were really nothing there, you'd see a signal this strong by pure chance only about three times in a thousand. But here's what the headlines leave out. In physics, three-sigma is not a discovery. The accepted gold standard for claiming something is real is five-sigma — that's the bar that confirmed the Higgs boson. Three-sigma, by contrast, is the kind of hint that earns a careful footnote, not a certainty. And the history of science is full of three-sigma signals that felt thrilling — and then simply vanished when more data arrived. Even Madhusudhan's own team admitted the significance was, in their words, at the lower end of what counts as robust evidence.

And then the rest of the field went to work. This is the part the viral posts skipped entirely.

One independent team reanalyzed the same Webb observations and confirmed methane at four-sigma — a genuinely solid detection — but found no statistically significant evidence for DMS at all. Another analysis, combining all of Webb's instruments across the full spectrum, concluded there was "insufficient evidence" for dimethyl sulfide or its cousin — at a meaningful level, the signal simply wasn't there. A separate paper put it bluntly, right in its title: K2-18b does not meet the standards of evidence for life.

Then came perhaps the most important check of all. A team led by a NASA scientist, Renyu Hu, gathered new Webb observations — several more passes of the planet crossing in front of its star — and pooled them with the originals. Then three separate groups analyzed the combined data independently. Their conclusion: K2-18b really is rich in water-related chemistry — but the possible detection of DMS was not statistically significant.

And here's the detail that quietly tells the whole story. That original signal turned out to depend on a technical choice — how the raw data was grouped together, or "binned." When researchers tried a different, arguably better way of binning the very same measurements, the DMS signal mostly disappeared. In one analysis, around seven out of every eight runs no longer favored DMS at all. A biosignature that changes depending on how you slice the spreadsheet isn't a biosignature. It's a maybe.

But let's steelman the other side, because honesty cuts both ways. Madhusudhan's team has not backed down. They argue their mid-infrared data captures something the other analyses underweight — and they're right that only more telescope time will truly settle it. This is a real, open, testable question, not a closed case. Webb keeps looking.

There's just one more problem — and it's the big one. Suppose the DMS is real. Would that even mean life? For years, the assumed answer was yes, because on Earth DMS comes from biology. But in just the last couple of years, that assumption cracked. DMS has now been detected in the gas around comet 67P — a lifeless ball of ice and dust — by the Rosetta spacecraft. It's been found drifting in interstellar space. And laboratory experiments simulating a young, lifeless planet have produced it from pure chemistry, with no biology at all. So even in the best case — even if the gas is truly there — dimethyl sulfide is no longer a smoking gun for life. It's a molecule that life can make… and so can a dead comet.

So what would it actually take? A real detection of life would need far more than a faint trace of one molecule. It would need a strong signal — five-sigma, not three — ideally of several gases at once, in a mix that no lifeless chemistry can easily fake, on a world we actually understand. Measured against that bar, K2-18b simply isn't there. Not yet, and not close.

And it's worth being honest about why this story travelled so far, so fast. We — all of us — want it to be true. After thousands of years of looking up and wondering whether we're alone, the idea that a telescope finally caught the scent of alien life is almost irresistible. That hope is one of the best things about us. It's also exactly why we have to be this careful — because the universe does not care what we want to find.

[MID-ROLL 2]

---

[SEGMENT 3: THE VERDICT]

So — signal, or noise?

Here's the honest answer. Every strong version of this claim has weakened under scrutiny. The "alien signal" — the dimethyl sulfide — has not survived independent analysis; multiple teams, using the same data and more, find no statistically significant detection. The number behind the headline, three-sigma, was never a discovery to begin with. The ocean world it supposedly lives on is itself unproven. And even if every piece held up, the gas isn't the proof of life it was sold as.

This isn't a cover-up, and it isn't a failure. It's science doing exactly what it's meant to do — a bold claim, made in the open, tested hard, and so far, not holding up. So the meter lands here: Mostly Noise. The headlines ran far ahead of the data.

But — and this matters — not life does not mean not extraordinary. K2-18b is still one of the most fascinating worlds we have ever studied. For the first time in history, we have a telescope that can read the air of a planet a hundred and twenty-four light-years away, and argue about its chemistry, molecule by molecule. We are genuinely learning how to search for life between the stars. We just haven't found it. Not yet.
